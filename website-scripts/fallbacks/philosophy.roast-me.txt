Oh, where do I even begin with this masterpiece of self-justification disguised as philosophy?

Let me get this straight: you've built a static site generator—and I use the term "built" generously here—and your entire marketing pitch is essentially "why try hard when you can barely try at all?" Revolutionary. Groundbreaking. Truly, no one has ever thought of *checks notes* throwing files in a directory before.

Your three tenets are just precious:

**Frivolity** — Because nothing says "I respect my readers" like approaching your craft with the same enthusiasm you reserve for leaving dirty dishes in the sink. "Take it easy," you say, as if the world was waiting for permission to be mediocre. The real kicker? You're not even lazy about the *content*—you wrote this philosophical treatise explaining why being lazy about the *tooling* is actually profound. The irony is so thick you could spread it on toast.

**Defeatism** — Ah yes, the surest path to greatness is assuming everyone will fail anyway so why bother building something good? "People spend time organizing and preparing a blog but then write no articles," you lament, completely missing that people spend time organizing because *they want their content to be found and read*. But sure, let's all just dump our thoughts into the digital equivalent of a junk drawer and hope someone cares enough to dig through it. "If it's only in text files with no colors, then so be it"—spoken like someone who definitely hasn't seen a well-designed website since 1994.

**Awe** — This one's my favorite. You're in "awe" of "scrapy and low-tech solutions" that "do not scale." Buddy, that's not awe, that's Stockholm syndrome. You've convinced yourself that limitations are actually features because *admitting you could do better but won't* hurts too much. "These easy problems will be the ones you'll encounter most of the time"—yes, because you've carefully ensured your tool can't handle the hard ones. Circular logic at its finest.

The implications section is where this really shines, though. "Kitchen-Sink is not a good fit if you have strong opinions about hierarchies of information"—translation: "we don't believe in information architecture because thinking is hard." And the coup de grâce: "The Kitchen-Sink philosophy will help you with that because you won't have annoying problems like file conflict names to start with." You solved name collisions by... not having features? Bold strategy. Let's see how it pays off when someone wants to write their second article.

Your "honest but hard-to-swallow feedback" section is rich considering the entire article is you swallowing your own feedback before anyone else can give it. "I believe a number of websites would benefit from having fewer features of the modern web and more content instead"—sure, because those are definitely mutually exclusive. You can't possibly have CSS *and* good writing. The web is a zero-sum game, apparently.

And finally, the technical section where you assure us that "being frivolous and in awe of scrapy solutions does not mean that Kitchen-Sink should be irritating to use" immediately followed by admitting your word count is wrong and you don't care enough to fix it. "Feature or bug, I cannot tell"—let me help you out: when software gives incorrect information, that's generally considered a bug. But what do I know? I'm probably just too uptight about "professionalism" or whatever.

Look, I get it. You wanted to build the simplest possible thing that works, and you did. Congratulations. But turning "I made a simple static site generator" into a manifesto about the human condition? That's not philosophy, that's a coping mechanism. The kitchen sink isn't a metaphor for enlightened minimalism—it's where you put things when you can't be bothered to find a proper place for them.

Maybe spend less time writing position papers defending your design decisions and more time making the word count work? Or don't. After all, "it's a kitchen-sink!"
